Food labels are written to satisfy regulators, not to inform people in a grocery aisle holding two cereal boxes. The information is technically all there — in six-point type, per-100g columns, and ingredient names that need a chemistry degree. [Etiqa Health](https://etiqahealth.com) exists to translate.

## What it does

Point your camera at a product and Etiqa returns an EtiScore with the reasoning in plain language: what drags the score down, what earns it back, and how the product compares against the alternative next to it on the shelf. It [launched on the iOS App Store](https://www.etiqahealth.com/blog/ios-launch.html) with a catalog of 55,000+ products from Panamanian supermarkets — local brands, not just imports — and is live today on iOS and Android.

## Built bilingual, on purpose

Most labels on Panamanian shelves are in Spanish, so an English-only scanner would miss the point. The [Etiqa 1.1 release](https://www.etiqahealth.com/blog/etiqa-1-1.html) brought full nutrition panels and ingredient insights in Spanish — the same English-and-Spanish posture the studio itself operates under. The product changelog lives on the [Etiqa blog](https://etiqahealth.com), where every release gets written up.

## Why the score is explainable

A bare number teaches nobody anything. Etiqa always shows its reasoning in plain language — what drags a score down, what earns it back — because the goal is that after a few weeks of scanning, you need the app less, not more. The scores are informational, not medical advice, and we keep that line clear on purpose.

## Why a systems studio ships consumer apps

Because shipping is the proof. Taking Etiqa from product spec to App Store release exercised every skill we sell to [client engagements](/#work) — and a studio that ships its own products end-to-end doesn't have to ask you to take rigor on faith.
